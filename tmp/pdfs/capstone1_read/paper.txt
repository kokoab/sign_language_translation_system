                                    CHAPTER 1


       This chapter presents the background of the study on the development of

ATLAS, an application-based Squeezeformer–Flan-T5 system for sign language

recognition   and English    translation. It discusses existing systems, recent

advancements in artificial intelligence and deep learning, and the limitations of

current approaches in practical communication settings. The chapter also outlines the

purpose, objectives, scope, and limitations of the study, highlighting the need for a

more accessible and efficient communication support system.


                                    Introduction


       Communication is a fundamental human need and a key element of inclusion

in educational and social environments. However, communication barriers continue to

exist between sign language users and people who do not understand sign language.

According to the World Health Organization, hearing loss affects a substantial portion

of the global population and can limit access to education, employment, healthcare,

and everyday social participation when communication support is inadequate (World

Health Organization, 2026). In many academic settings, interactions between sign

language users and non-sign language users are often reduced to written notes,

improvised gestures, or delayed text-based exchanges rather than direct and efficient

communication.


       Recent developments in artificial intelligence, computer vision, and natural

language processing have created new opportunities for sign language recognition and

translation systems. One important development is the use of landmark-based visual
representations instead of relying solely on raw video frames. Landmark-based input

allows a system to focus on meaningful positional and motion cues while reducing

unnecessary visual background information. In this study, the extraction phase is

designed around landmark-based hand and body pose detection, with Apple Vision

used as the primary extraction engine in the current evaluated implementation and

MediaPipe considered as a cross-platform extraction alternative. MediaPipe Hands

was designed as an on-device real-time hand tracking pipeline that predicts hand

skeletons from monocular RGB input through a palm detector and hand landmark

model, while Apple’s Vision framework provides built-in capabilities for detecting

hand and human body poses in images and video on supported devices (Bazarevsky et

al., 2020; Apple, n.d.-a; Apple, n.d.-b).


        Despite these advances, sign language recognition is not simply a matter of

detecting hand shapes or body positions. Signed languages are natural languages with

their own linguistic structure, grammar, and visual-spatial organization, which means

they cannot be treated as direct word-for-word equivalents of spoken or written

English. Because of these structural differences, sign language translation requires

more than accurate gesture recognition; it also requires a system that can generate

grammatically coherent and meaningful English output from recognized sign

sequences (Camgoz et al., 2020; Müller et al., 2023). Prior work has also shown that

intermediate representations such as glosses can play an important role in bridging

recognition and translation stages in sign language processing (Camgoz et al., 2020).


        For sequential recognition tasks, modern deep learning architectures

increasingly combine self-attention and convolution in order to model both long-range

dependencies and local patterns. The Conformer architecture demonstrated the

effectiveness of this hybrid design by combining Transformer-based global context

modeling with convolution-based local feature extraction in a parameter-efficient way

(Gulati et al., 2020). Building on this idea, Squeezeformer was introduced as a more

efficient Transformer architecture that redesigns the Conformer structure through a

Temporal U-Net, simplified block organization, removal of redundant normalization

steps, and efficient depthwise subsampling. In its original study, Squeezeformer

consistently outperformed comparable automatic speech recognition baselines under

the same training schemes while maintaining computational efficiency (Kim et al.,

2022). Although Squeezeformer was originally proposed for automatic speech

recognition, its sequence-modeling design makes it a strong candidate for sign

language recognition tasks that also require efficient modeling of local and global

temporal patterns (Gulati et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2022).


        For language generation, Flan-T5 provides a strong foundation for converting

structured intermediate sign or gloss representations into natural English text. Flan-T5

is an instruction-tuned variant of the Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer, which frames

language problems in a unified text-to-text format and allows tasks such as translation

and generation to be handled within one sequence-to-sequence framework (Raffel et

al., 2020; Chung et al., 2024). This makes Flan-T5 suitable for gloss-to-English

generation because the model can process structured sign or gloss-like input and

generate     readable,     grammatically      understandable       English       output.   The

instruction-tuned nature of Flan-T5 is particularly relevant in sign language

translation because it supports prompt-based and contextual generation rather than

simple word substitution, which is necessary when translating signed language

structures into natural English sentences (Raffel et al., 2020; Camgoz et al., 2020;

Chung et al., 2024).

        Despite continued progress in sign language technologies, many existing

systems still face limitations in practical deployment. Some models are

computationally demanding, some focus only on recognition without producing

natural-language output, and others are developed primarily for benchmark settings

rather than real communication scenarios. These limitations highlight the need for a

more accessible and practical assistive system that can capture sign input, recognize

sequential gesture patterns efficiently, and generate understandable English text for

non-sign language users. In response to this need, the present study proposes ATLAS:

An    Application-Based      Squeezeformer–Flan-T5         System     for   Sign   Language

Recognition and English Translation. The system uses landmark-based extraction,

Squeezeformer-based sign recognition, a CTC-based continuous recognition stage,

and a Flan-T5-based language generation model for English translation. Through this

pipeline, the study aims to contribute a practical assistive communication tool that

translates recognized sign language input into understandable English text.


1.2 Purpose and Description


        The purpose of this study is to design, develop, and evaluate ATLAS: An

Application-Based Squeezeformer–Flan-T5 System for Sign Language Recognition

and English Translation. The proposed system aims to assist non-sign language users

in understanding signed messages by recognizing sign language input and translating

it into coherent and grammatically understandable English text. In doing so, the study

seeks to help reduce communication barriers between sign language users and the

hearing population within academic and social environments, particularly at Leyte

Normal University.

        This study focuses on evaluating the performance of the proposed system in

terms of recognition accuracy, translation quality, and suitability for application-based

deployment. In the recognition stage, the system adopts Squeezeformer as the main

sequence-modeling backbone for sign language recognition. Squeezeformer was

originally introduced as an efficient Transformer architecture for automatic speech

recognition and was shown to perform strongly under comparable computational

settings through a design that combines self-attention and convolution while reducing

unnecessary architectural overhead (Kim et al., 2022). Its architecture incorporates

efficiency-oriented refinements such as simplified block design, reduced redundancy,

and efficient temporal downsampling, making it a relevant backbone for tasks that

require accurate and efficient temporal modeling (Kim et al., 2022).


        The recognition process is further supported by a CTC-based continuous

recognition stage. This stage allows the system to process phrase-level input and

generate sign or gloss sequences before translation. This is important because sign

language translation requires more than isolated sign classification; the system must

also account for ordered sign sequences that represent a complete message.


        In the final translation stage, the system uses Flan-T5 to convert recognized

sign or gloss sequences into natural English text. Flan-T5 is an instruction-tuned

variant of the Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer, an encoder-decoder Transformer

model that frames natural language processing tasks under a unified text-to-text

paradigm (Raffel et al., 2020). This makes Flan-T5 suitable for sign-to-English

translation because the model can generate coherent English output from structured

textual input while also benefiting from instruction tuning. Since the system processes

recognized sign or gloss-like sequences, Flan-T5 supports the generation of

isolated sign labels (Chung et al., 2024).


        The developed system is not intended to function as a fully bidirectional

communication system in which both parties directly operate the interface. Rather, it

is designed primarily as an assistive translation tool for non-sign language users, who

serve as the direct users of the system by viewing the translated English output and,

when necessary, entering a typed response. In this setup, the sign language user serves

mainly as the source of gesture input that the system captures and processes.


        In addition, the system is not designed to perform simultaneous live translation

while the sign language user is still signing. Instead, it follows a turn-based or

segment-based translation process in which the completed sign input or phrase

segment is captured and processed before the corresponding English text is generated.

Because of this workflow, the system is more accurately described as a turn-based

sign language recognition and translation system rather than a fully real-time

interpreter.


        To evaluate the developed system, a structured ASL dataset of predefined sign

language gestures, continuous phrase samples, and gloss-to-English pairs is

assembled and used for training, testing, recognition, and Flan-T5-based translation.

These samples allow the recognition model to learn relevant sign patterns and enable

the translation model to generate understandable English output. In addition, ISO/IEC

25010:2023 is adopted as the software quality evaluation framework for assessing the

system’s functional suitability, usability, reliability, and performance efficiency within

an academic assistive communication setting.

1.4 Scope and Limitations


        This study focuses on the design, development, and evaluation of ATLAS: An

Application-Based Squeezeformer–Flan-T5 System for Sign Language Recognition

and English Translation. The proposed system is built around landmark-based feature

extraction, a Squeezeformer-based recognition backbone, a CTC-based continuous

recognition stage, and a final sign-to-English translation stage utilizing a pretrained

Flan-T5 model. In the evaluated implementation, Apple Vision serves as the primary

landmark extraction engine, while MediaPipe is included as a cross-platform

extraction alternative for future deployment. The system is intended to recognize

predefined ASL signs and selected phrase-level inputs, generate sign or gloss

sequences, and convert them into understandable English text to assist non-sign

language users in interpreting signed messages. The current implementation is

turn-based or segment-based and assistive in nature rather than a fully simultaneous

real-time interpreter.


        The study is limited to a structured dataset of predefined gestures, sign-label

sequences, continuous phrase samples, and gloss-to-English pairs assembled for

training, testing, and evaluation. Although the dataset contains a substantial number of

extracted samples, it remains limited in signer diversity, variation, and environmental

coverage. As a result, the model may have difficulty recognizing unfamiliar signs,

unseen variations, or inputs captured under uncontrolled conditions. The performance

of the system therefore depends heavily on the quality, consistency, and diversity of

the available data. In addition, the current evaluation setup is class-stratified rather

than signer-stratified, which means the reported recognition performance should be

interpreted as signer-dependent rather than fully signer-independent.


        Another limitation of the study is that the system is restricted to American

Sign Language (ASL) only. The dataset, sign classes, sign-label sequences, and

recognition pipeline used in the present study are all based on ASL, which means the

system is not designed to recognize or translate other sign languages such as Filipino

Sign Language (FSL) or other regional sign language systems. Because sign

languages differ in vocabulary, grammar, structure, and gesture formation, the current

model cannot be assumed to generalize across other sign languages without separate

datasets, retraining, and evaluation. This limitation should therefore be considered

when interpreting the scope and applicability of the proposed system.


        A further limitation of the study is that the system does not yet perform true

simultaneous live translation. Instead of generating English output continuously while

the sign language user is still signing, the system follows a turn-based or

segment-based translation process in which the full sign input or phrase segment is

        The system is also designed and optimized for future mobile deployment, but

contextual signing cues, and natural coarticulation patterns that may affect meaning in

real-world sign language communication. These limitations should be considered

when interpreting the current performance of the proposed system.

                REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND SYSTEMS

         This    chapter    reviews     existing   studies,   systems, and technological

developments       relevant    to     the    proposed   ATLAS:     An   Application-Based

Squeezeformer-T5 System for Sign Language Recognition and English Translation. It

discusses the theoretical foundations of sign language recognition, landmark-based

visual    representation,     sequence modeling,        Squeezeformer-based   recognition,

intermediate sign or gloss representation, and T5-based English text generation. By

examining previous methods, architectures, and system limitations, this review

identifies the research and implementation gaps that justify the development of a

mobile-based, turn-based assistive system for translating recognized sign language

input into grammatically understandable English text.



2.1 Related Literature

2.1.1 Natural Language Processing and Sign Language Translation

         Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a critical component of modern sign

language translation because the task extends beyond recognizing visible gestures and

requires converting signed input into meaningful spoken or written language output.

Recent work argues that signed languages should be treated as full natural languages

within NLP because they exhibit core linguistic properties such as structure, grammar,

and compositional meaning, yet they are still often underrepresented in mainstream

NLP research (Yin et al., 2021). This view is important in sign language translation

because the goal is not only to identify signs correctly, but also to generate output that

is understandable and linguistically appropriate in the target language. Camgoz et al.
(2020) showed that continuous sign language recognition and translation can be

learned jointly within a Transformer-based framework, reinforcing the idea that sign

language processing should be addressed as a sequence-to-sequence language

problem rather than as isolated gesture classification alone.


        A central issue in sign language translation is the structural mismatch between

signed and spoken languages. Sign languages do not simply follow the word order of

spoken English, and direct word-for-word substitution often fails to preserve meaning.

This motivates the use of language-aware translation models that can capture context

and generate more natural output. In the literature, sign language translation is

increasingly framed as a machine translation problem in which the system learns to

map signed input representations into spoken-language sentences. Camgoz et al.

(2020) demonstrated that Transformer-based sign language translation can jointly

model recognition and translation through CTC-guided learning, while Yin et al.

(2021) emphasized that linguistic structure should be taken seriously when modeling

signed languages computationally. These findings support the view that NLP is not

optional in sign language translation systems but is instead necessary for producing

coherent English text from recognized sign sequences.


        For the present study, this literature provides the theoretical basis for treating

ATLAS as both a recognition system and a translation system. The system is not

intended merely to classify signs; it is also designed to generate English text output

that can be understood by non-sign language users. Because of this, the translation

stage must be grounded in NLP principles that account for sequence context,

linguistic structure, and target-language generation. This makes the inclusion of a

dedicated language generation component academically and practically justified.

2.1.2 Landmark-Based Visual Representation for Sign Language Recognition

        Landmark-based visual representation has become an important approach in

sign language recognition because it provides a structured alternative to raw video

processing. Instead of analyzing every pixel in a frame, landmark-based methods

represent relevant body parts through coordinate points, allowing the model to focus

on movement and configuration patterns that are more directly related to gesture

interpretation. This can reduce sensitivity to background clutter and can make

processing more efficient for application-oriented systems. MediaPipe Hands is a

well-known example of this approach: Zhang et al. (2020) described it as an

on-device real-time hand tracking pipeline that predicts a hand skeleton from a single

RGB camera using a palm detector followed by a hand landmark model. The same

work reports real-time inference on mobile GPUs and high-quality landmark

prediction, making it relevant to lightweight gesture-based applications.


        In addition to MediaPipe, Apple’s Vision framework provides built-in pose

analysis tools that are relevant to sign input extraction. Apple’s documentation states

that Vision supports hand pose detection and human body pose detection in images

and video, allowing applications to identify key points associated with hands and

body structure on supported Apple platforms (Apple, n.d.-a; Apple, n.d.-b). This is

important because sign language is not expressed through hand shape alone;

upper-body posture, arm movement, and visible body alignment can also contribute to

the meaning and clarity of signing. The availability of both hand pose and body pose

estimation capabilities therefore strengthens the extraction phase of a sign language

system by enabling a richer visual representation of the signer’s movement.


___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.    | 13
        For ATLAS, landmark-based extraction is especially suitable because the

system is designed as an application-based assistive tool rather than as a large

raw-video benchmark model. By using MediaPipe and Apple Vision, the study adopts

a practical extraction strategy that emphasizes structured hand and body information

while remaining computationally lighter than full-frame visual modeling. This

literature supports the decision to use coordinate-based visual input as the foundation

for the later recognition and translation stages of the proposed system.



2.1.3 Sequence Modeling for Sign Language Recognition

        Sign language recognition is inherently a sequence modeling problem because

the meaning of a sign often depends on how movement unfolds over time rather than

on a single static pose. Temporal order, motion direction, rhythm, and transitions

between positions all contribute to interpretation, particularly in continuous signing.

For this reason, models that only examine isolated frames or short local features may

fail to capture the broader context needed for accurate recognition and translation.

Recent sign language research increasingly reflects this perspective by modeling sign

input as a temporal sequence rather than as a set of unrelated snapshots. Camgoz et al.

(2020), for example, proposed a joint end-to-end framework for continuous sign

language recognition and translation that explicitly treats the input as a sequence and

binds recognition and translation through a CTC objective.


        This shift toward sequence-aware modeling parallels wider trends in deep

learning, where attention-based architectures have shown strong ability to capture

long-range dependencies across sequential data. In the sign language domain, such

architectures are useful because the interpretation of one segment of a sign sequence

may depend on earlier or later segments. Camgoz et al. (2020) showed that a
___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.    | 14
Transformer-based framework can model these dependencies effectively, which

helped establish sequence modeling as a central principle in modern sign language

translation. The literature therefore supports the view that recognition in sign

language systems should not be limited to static gesture identification, but should

instead preserve temporal relationships across the input stream.


        In the present study, this literature justifies the use of a recognition backbone

specifically chosen for sequential processing. Since ATLAS is expected to process

ordered landmark sequences and convert them into meaningful output, the model

must be able to learn both local motion cues and broader temporal context. This

makes sequence modeling a fundamental requirement of the system rather than a

secondary enhancement.



2.1.4 Squeezeformer as an Efficient Recognition Architecture

        Hybrid attention-convolution architectures have become influential in

sequence modeling because they combine the ability of attention mechanisms to

capture long-range dependencies with the ability of convolutional modules to model

local structure. Within this line of research, Squeezeformer was introduced by Kim et

al. (2022) as an efficient Transformer architecture for automatic speech recognition.

The authors report that the model improves on Conformer-like designs through a

Temporal U-Net structure, a simpler block organization, removal of redundant Layer

Normalization operations, and an efficient depthwise down-sampling layer. They

further report that their final Squeezeformer model outperformed a comparable

Conformer baseline while maintaining the same FLOP budget, supporting its

efficiency claims.


___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.      | 15
        Although Squeezeformer was proposed in the speech domain, its architectural

characteristics are relevant to other sequential recognition tasks because it is designed

to capture both local and global patterns efficiently. This is especially significant for

sign language recognition, where temporal dependencies matter but computational

practicality is also important for deployment-oriented systems. The defensible claim

from the literature is therefore not that Squeezeformer is already established

specifically   for   sign    language     recognition,    but   that    it   is an efficient

sequence-modeling architecture whose design principles are suitable for temporally

structured input. That distinction is important for academic accuracy, especially when

adapting architectures across domains.


        For ATLAS, Squeezeformer provides the theoretical basis for the recognition

stage because the system requires a model that can process landmark sequences

efficiently while retaining important temporal information. Its efficiency-oriented

design aligns with the application-based goal of the study, and its ability to balance

local and long-range modeling makes it an appropriate backbone for sign sequence

recognition within the proposed architecture.



2.1.5 T5 and Intermediate Representation in Sign Language Translation

        The Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer (T5) is a major sequence-to-sequence

language model that frames diverse NLP tasks within a unified text-to-text

formulation.    Raffel et al. (2020) showed that translation, summarization,

classification, and other language tasks can all be expressed as text generation

problems under one common architecture. This is relevant to sign language translation

because recognized sign outputs can be represented in textual form and then

converted into natural English through a sequence-to-sequence model. T5 therefore
___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.          | 16
provides a strong language-generation basis for systems that aim to move from

recognition into coherent target-language output.


        In sign language translation research, intermediate representations such as

gloss remain important because they help bridge the gap between visual sign input

and spoken-language generation. Camgoz et al. (2020) note that prior work had

shown gloss-level tokenization to be highly beneficial for translation performance,

and their Transformer-based framework continued to reflect the importance of

intermediate structure in sign language translation. Similarly, Moryossef et al. (2021)

describe gloss-to-text translation as a low-resource neural machine translation

problem and show that sign language translation is often decomposed into

video-to-gloss recognition and gloss-to-text translation. These studies support the idea

that a staged pipeline can be more practical and effective than forcing the system to

learn direct end-to-end translation from visual input alone.


        At the same time, more recent work has also explored gloss-free translation

approaches. Lin et al. (2023) argue that while intermediate representations like gloss

have proven effective, gloss annotations are difficult to obtain in large quantities,

which can limit dataset coverage and real-world scalability. Their gloss-free

framework aims to reduce dependence on explicit gloss labels while still exploiting

shared semantics between signs and spoken translation. This newer direction does not

eliminate the value of intermediate representation; rather, it highlights an ongoing

trade-off between annotation cost, interpretability, and modeling flexibility. In the

context of the present study, intermediate recognized representation remains a

reasonable and well-supported design choice because it offers a practical bridge from

recognition to English generation while maintaining modularity in the pipeline.

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.     | 17
        Taken together, the literature supports the use of T5 as the final language

generation component of ATLAS and supports the broader idea of using an

intermediate recognition output before producing English text. T5 contributes the

text-generation capacity needed for fluent target-language output, while intermediate

representation provides a structured handoff between the visual recognition stage and

the translation stage. This makes the translation component of the proposed system

consistent with both established and emerging directions in sign language NLP

research.



2.1 Related Systems

2.2.1 Vision-Based Sign Language Recognition Systems


        Vision-based sign language recognition systems use cameras, images, or video

input to identify signed gestures. These systems are important because they allow sign

input to be captured without requiring wearable sensors, gloves, or specialized

hardware. In most camera-based systems, the signer performs a gesture in front of a

camera, and the system analyzes visual information such as hand shape, hand

movement, body posture, and spatial position. This makes vision-based recognition

more practical for ordinary users because the system can operate using common

devices with built-in cameras.


        A major related system in this area is the Sign Language Transformers

framework by Camgoz et al. (2020), which introduced a Transformer-based

architecture for continuous sign language recognition and translation. Their system

jointly learned sign language recognition and translation by using a Connectionist

Temporal Classification loss to connect the recognition and translation tasks. This


___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.   | 18
shows that sign language systems should not be limited to identifying isolated

gestures, because continuous signing requires sequence-level understanding and

language translation. Their study also reported strong performance on the

RWTH-PHOENIX-Weather-2014T                dataset,     showing      the    effectiveness     of

Transformer-based architectures for sign language recognition and translation.


        This system is related to ATLAS because both systems treat sign language as a

sequence-based recognition and translation problem rather than as simple image

classification. However, ATLAS differs in its intended deployment. While Sign

Language Transformers was developed mainly as a research and benchmark-oriented

framework, ATLAS is designed as a mobile-based assistive application for practical

communication support. ATLAS also uses landmark-based extraction before

recognition, which can reduce unnecessary background information and make the

system more suitable for application-based deployment. This makes ATLAS more

focused on practical use by non-sign language users who need readable English

output after a completed sign input.



2.2.2 Landmark-Based and Keypoint-Based Recognition Systems


        Landmark-based recognition systems use extracted body points, hand points,

or skeletal coordinates instead of relying on full raw video frames. This approach is

useful for sign language recognition because signs are strongly connected to the

movement and position of hands, arms, and upper body. By converting visual input

into coordinate-based data, the system can focus on meaningful gesture features while

reducing background noise, lighting variation, and irrelevant visual details.




___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.             | 19
        One important system that supports this approach is MediaPipe Hands by

Zhang et al. (2020). MediaPipe Hands is an on-device, real-time hand tracking

pipeline that predicts a hand skeleton from a single RGB camera input. It uses a palm

detector followed by a hand landmark model, making it useful for applications that

need lightweight and structured hand tracking. Apple’s Vision framework also

supports this direction because it provides built-in capabilities for detecting hand

poses and human body poses in images and video. Apple’s documentation describes

Vision as a framework for analyzing images and video, including body pose tracking

for action and gesture recognition.


        These systems are directly related to ATLAS because ATLAS uses MediaPipe

and Apple Vision in the extraction phase. Instead of processing every pixel of the

video, ATLAS uses landmark data as the visual representation of the sign input. This

supports the system’s goal of being mobile-based and assistive, since landmark

extraction can be lighter and more focused than raw-frame processing.


        ATLAS improves on basic landmark-based recognition systems because it

does not stop at detecting hand or body points. MediaPipe and Apple Vision mainly

provide pose or landmark extraction; they do not automatically produce full sign

language translation. ATLAS uses these landmark outputs as the first stage of a larger

pipeline. After extracting coordinates, ATLAS applies a Squeezeformer-based

recognition model to analyze temporal sign patterns and then uses T5 to generate

English text. Therefore, ATLAS builds upon landmark detection systems by turning

extracted keypoints into a complete recognition-and-translation workflow.


        The reason this model was chosen is that landmark-based extraction is more

aligned with the study’s mobile and turn-based nature. Since ATLAS is not intended

___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.   | 20
to be a high-cost raw-video benchmark system, using landmarks helps make the input

representation more structured, efficient, and suitable for application deployment.




2.2.3 Deep Learning-Based Sequential Sign Recognition Systems


        Deep learning-based sequential recognition systems are designed to process

ordered input over time. This is important in sign language because signs are not only

defined by hand shape but also by movement, direction, timing, and transitions. A

single frame may not be enough to understand a sign; the model must analyze how the

gesture changes from one moment to another.


        Transformer-based systems have become important in this field because they

can model long-range dependencies in sequential data. Camgoz et al. (2020)

demonstrated this through a Transformer-based framework that jointly handled

continuous sign language recognition and translation. Their study supports the idea

that sign language recognition should preserve temporal relationships and should be

connected to translation rather than isolated gesture classification.


        ATLAS is related to these sequential recognition systems because it also treats

sign input as a temporal sequence. However, instead of using a standard Transformer

as its recognition backbone, ATLAS uses Squeezeformer. Squeezeformer was

introduced by Kim et al. (2022) as an efficient Transformer architecture for automatic

speech recognition. It improves on Conformer-like designs through architectural

changes such as Temporal U-Net structure, simplified block organization, removal of

redundant normalization operations, and efficient depthwise downsampling. The




___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.        | 21
Squeezeformer paper reported that it outperformed comparable ASR baselines under

the same training schemes while maintaining computational efficiency.


        Although Squeezeformer was originally developed for speech recognition, its

design is relevant to ATLAS because both speech and sign recognition involve

sequential patterns. The defensible claim is not that Squeezeformer is already the

standard model for sign language recognition, but that its efficient sequence-modeling

structure makes it a suitable backbone for processing ordered landmark sequences.

This is important for ATLAS because the system needs to recognize local motion

cues, such as small hand movements, while also understanding broader temporal

context across the completed sign input.


        ATLAS is better suited to the study’s goals than heavier or more general

sequential systems because Squeezeformer supports efficient temporal modeling.

Since ATLAS is intended for mobile-based, turn-based application use, the model

must balance recognition performance with practical deployment concerns.

Squeezeformer was chosen because it provides a stronger fit for this balance: it is

sequence-aware like Transformer-based models, but its architecture is designed to

reduce unnecessary computational overhead.



2.2.4 Gloss-Based or Intermediate Representation Translation Systems


        Gloss-based and intermediate-representation systems divide sign language

translation into stages. Instead of translating directly from video to spoken or written

language, the system first recognizes signs into a structured representation, such as

glosses or sign labels, and then translates that representation into a natural-language

sentence. This is important because sign languages have their own grammar and

___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.     | 22
structure, so direct word-for-word conversion into English often produces unnatural

or incorrect output.


        Moryossef et al. (2021) described sign language translation as often being

decomposed into video-to-gloss recognition and gloss-to-text translation. Their study

treated gloss-to-text translation as a low-resource neural machine translation problem

and showed that additional synthetic training data could improve translation

performance for ASL-to-English and DGS-to-German. Camgoz et al. (2020) also

emphasized the importance of gloss-level representation in sign language translation

and used recognition and translation together within a Transformer-based system.


        ATLAS is related to these systems because it also follows a staged pipeline.

The system first recognizes sign input through the Squeezeformer-based recognition

stage, then uses a T5-based translation stage to generate English text. This staged

design is appropriate because it provides a structured handoff between visual

recognition and language generation. It also makes the system more modular,

meaning the recognition and translation components can be improved separately in

future versions.


        ATLAS improves on simple gloss-based systems by using a modern

text-to-text language model for the final English generation stage. T5, introduced by

Raffel et al. (2020), frames different NLP tasks under a unified text-to-text format,

making it suitable for translation and generation tasks. In ATLAS, this is useful

because the recognized sign or gloss-like output must be converted into

grammatically understandable English rather than displayed only as isolated labels.


        However, it is important to acknowledge that recent systems have also

explored gloss-free sign language translation. Lin et al. (2023) argued that gloss
___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.     | 23
annotations are difficult to obtain in large quantities, which can limit real-world

dataset coverage. Their GloFE framework addressed gloss-free sign language

translation by using shared semantic concepts between signs and spoken translations.

This shows that gloss-free systems are an emerging direction. Still, ATLAS uses an

intermediate representation because it is more practical for the current study’s

structured dataset, predefined ASL signs, and modular application-based design.


        Therefore, ATLAS is better for this study’s scope because it chooses a more

manageable and explainable pipeline. Rather than attempting a fully end-to-end

gloss-free system that would require larger datasets and more complex training,

ATLAS uses recognition followed by T5-based English generation. This makes the

system easier to develop, evaluate, and defend within the limits of the capstone

project.



2.2.5 Application-Based or Mobile Sign Language Translation Systems


        Application-based or mobile sign language translation systems focus on

making recognition and translation usable outside laboratory or benchmark

environments. These systems are important because the real value of sign language

technology depends not only on model accuracy but also on whether the system can

support actual communication. A practical system must consider user interaction,

device capability, processing time, usability, and output clarity.


        ATLAS belongs to this category because it is designed as a mobile-based

assistive communication application. According to the paper, the system is not

intended to function as a fully bidirectional live interpreter. Instead, it is designed

mainly for non-sign language users, who view the translated English output and may

___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.    | 24
enter a typed response when necessary. The sign language user provides the gesture

input, while the application processes the completed sign input and produces English

text through a turn-based workflow.


        This makes ATLAS different from systems that focus only on recognition

accuracy or benchmark performance. Some related systems may achieve strong

results in controlled datasets, but they do not always address practical deployment

issues such as mobile operation, user-facing output, typed response support, or

turn-based communication flow. ATLAS specifically defines these as part of its

functional and performance requirements, including keypoint extraction, sign gesture

recognition, gloss-to-English translation, text response support, mobile-based

operation, and turn-based processing.


        ATLAS is also related to MediaPipe-based and Apple Vision-based mobile

systems because these technologies are designed for practical visual analysis on

supported devices. MediaPipe Hands supports real-time on-device hand tracking from

a single RGB camera, while Apple Vision supports hand and body pose detection for

image and video analysis. These systems support ATLAS’s decision to use

landmark-based extraction in a mobile environment.


        The reason ATLAS chose this kind of model is that the system needs both

practicality and language output. A recognition-only mobile app would not be enough

because non-sign language users need understandable English, not only gesture labels.

A fully end-to-end raw-video translation model may also be too complex for the

study’s dataset and deployment scope. ATLAS therefore uses a balanced design:

landmark extraction for efficient visual input, Squeezeformer for temporal

recognition, and T5 for English generation. This makes the system more suitable for a

___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.   | 25
capstone-level mobile assistive tool because each component has a clear role and is

supported by existing research.

                          TECHNICAL BACKGROUND

       This chapter presents the technical foundation of the proposed system,

including the existing communication methods and the detailed architecture of the

developed sign language translation system. It explains how the system captures,

processes, and translates sign language gestures into meaningful English sentences.

Furthermore, it outlines the actual computational pipeline, including feature

3.1 Current System

       The existing communication landscape at Leyte Normal University (LNU) for

deaf and mute students relies heavily on traditional and manual methods.

Communication between deaf individuals and the hearing community—such as

instructors, staff, and peers—is commonly facilitated through handwritten notes or

basic mobile note-taking applications. While these approaches allow message

exchange, they lack real-time interaction and conversational efficiency.

       These methods introduce delays, especially in academic settings where

immediate responses are necessary. The manual transcription process often results in

shortened or simplified messages, limiting the expression of complex ideas.

Additionally, there is no system that bridges the linguistic differences between sign

language and English, leading to frequent misunderstandings.

       This setup places the burden of communication entirely on deaf students and

restricts spontaneous interactions in everyday scenarios. The absence of an automated
translation system highlights the need for a more efficient and real-time

communication solution.



3.2 Proposed System


        The proposed system, ATLAS (Application-Based Squeezeformer-T5 System

for Sign Language Recognition and English Translation), is a mobile-based sign

language    recognition     and    English    translation system developed using    a

PyTorch-based deep learning pipeline. It is designed to process skeletal landmark data

extracted from mobile device cameras and translate sign gestures into meaningful

English text. Unlike traditional image-based recognition systems that rely on raw

pixel data, the system uses coordinate-based representations enriched with kinematic

and geometric features, allowing more efficient on-device processing while

maintaining recognition performance.


        The system begins with a platform-adaptive feature extraction stage. For

Android-based deployments, MediaPipe is used as the primary landmark detection

backbone, providing structured whole-body and hand keypoints from sign language

video input. For iOS-based deployments, the Apple Vision Framework serves as the

primary keypoint extraction backbone, leveraging on-device body and hand pose

estimation capabilities. Both extractors provide structured skeletal data—including

hand and upper-body reference points—that are used as the foundation for

downstream recognition and translation. The extracted landmarks are then

transformed into structured feature representations, including coordinate-based and

geometric descriptors together with additional derived representations for motion and

skeletal relationships.

___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.   | 28
        The isolated-sign recognition stage operates on fixed-length input clips of 32

frames. For each clip, the system uses 61 landmark nodes composed of 21 left-hand

keypoints, 21 right-hand keypoints, 15 face keypoints, and 4 upper-body reference

points. Each node is represented by 16 input channels derived from spatial

coordinates, motion descriptors, visibility or masking information, and bone-related

features, resulting in an input tensor of shape [B, 32, 61, 16]. These specifications

define the exact structure of the skeletal input processed by the Squeezeformer

recognition backbone.


        Following feature extraction, the system performs sign recognition through the

Squeezeformer architecture, an efficient transformer-based model originally designed

for speech recognition that has been adapted in this study for skeletal gesture

sequence modeling. The Squeezeformer captures both spatial relationships among

detected landmarks and the temporal progression of gestures across sequential frames

through its time-reduction and convolution-augmented self-attention modules. This

design allows the system to model the structural configuration of hand and facial

movements alongside their motion dynamics, while maintaining computational

efficiency suitable for mobile deployment. In the implemented system, the model is

trained using ArcFace angular margin loss with scheduled scale and margin ramps,

mixup augmentation, and label smoothing. These techniques are used to improve

class separation and support more robust recognition across gesture patterns.


        The training dataset used in the system is composed of 66,451 extracted .npy

files generated from approximately 67,000 raw sign language videos, covering 310

ASL classes and 7 unique signers. These isolated-sign samples were assembled from

publicly available ASL video sources, including WLASL-derived videos, YouTube

___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.   | 29
ASL collections, and related ASL datasets, and were processed through

platform-consistent skeletal extraction to produce structured representations for model

training. In addition, Stage 2 sequence-level modeling uses 780 continuous phrase

videos across 9 phrases, which serve as the basis for continuous recognition and

gloss-sequence experiments.


        For continuous sign recognition, the system uses a Stage 2 CTC-based

architecture that extends the isolated-sign Squeezeformer backbone into phrase-level

processing. In this stage, encoded clip-level features are pooled and passed to a

sequence model that handles variable-length multi-clip input and predicts gloss

sequences through CTC decoding. This stage is trained using both synthetic phrase

construction from isolated-sign clips and a separate set of real continuous phrase

videos. Through this design, the system is able to move from single-sign classification

toward continuous phrase recognition before gloss-to-English translation.


        The final stage of the system involves translating recognized gloss sequences

into natural English text using the Flan-T5 model, a pretrained and instruction-tuned

transformer. This model converts structured gloss representations into grammatically

correct and contextually understandable English output. Through this stage, the

system is able to move beyond simple sign recognition and produce more meaningful

translated text for the Non-Sign Language User.


        During inference, the system operates through a mobile application pipeline

that uses MediaPipe on Android and Apple Vision on iOS for on-device video capture

and landmark extraction. The implementation is designed for mobile deployment,

supporting a turn-based sign language recognition and English translation workflow

on consumer-grade smartphones. Although the architecture is designed with
___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.    | 30
cross-platform portability in mind, the completed system in the present study is

described as a mobile-based, turn-based sign language recognition and English

translation tool.


        Overall, the proposed ATLAS system provides an efficient and structured

approach to sign language recognition and English translation by integrating

platform-adaptive     landmark      extraction,   Squeezeformer-based        spatial-temporal

modeling, CTC-based sequence recognition, and Flan-T5 natural language generation.

Through this multi-stage pipeline, the system is designed to support more effective

understanding of sign language input through English text output.

Systems Development Life Cycle

        The project utilized the Sashimi Model, originally proposed by DeGrace and

Stahl, which is characterized as a “waterfall model with overlapping phases” or “the

waterfall model with feedback.” Unlike the traditional waterfall approach, this model

allows information about problem areas to be addressed early because the phases

overlap, enabling implementation issues to be identified during the design stage. This

iterative method helps alleviate many problems of traditional models and is

theoretically effective at managing costs by limiting iterations to two adjacent phases

at a time, thereby preventing expensive backward moves from development all the

way back to requirements. In practice, this framework allowed the team to develop

the T5-based English translation component simultaneously with the refinement of the

Squeezeformer-based sign recognition model. This overlap helped ensure that the

___________________________________________________________________________

Batiancela, F. C., Cabidog, R. G., Lapid, K. G., Esio, J. A., & Funcion, D. D.           | 40
translation stage correctly interpreted the recognized output from the recognition

stage, allowing the team to immediately address errors such as the misidentification of

specific hand gestures or sequence-level prediction issues. This parallel workflow also

supported the development of a structured and efficient mobile-based assistive

translation system for the Leyte Normal University community. The cycle concluded

with White-box and Black-box testing aligned with ISO/IEC 25010:2023 standards to

help guarantee both technical and functional quality.

Design

         The    system     architecture    integrates     skeletal   landmark    extraction,

Squeezeformer-based sign recognition, and T5-based sign-to-English translation

within a modular pipeline for sign language understanding. The current

implementation follows a mobile-based, turn-based workflow intended to support

non-sign language users in understanding signed input through English text output. A

modular approach was employed to separate feature processing, recognition, and

translation, while still allowing future expansion for additional vocabularies, datasets,

and deployment environments.
