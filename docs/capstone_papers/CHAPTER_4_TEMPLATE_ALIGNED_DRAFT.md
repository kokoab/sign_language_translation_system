### Data Preparation and Separation

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Move current 4.3.1 here, retaining its annotation, data-separation and participant-related distinctions. Do not duplicate it under 4.4.

The development of ATLAS required examples representing individual vocabulary signs, connected signing and gloss-to-English generation. Isolated-sign clips supplied vocabulary labels, phrase recordings supplied examples of signs within sequences, and gloss-to-English pairs supplied sentence-generation targets. These inputs were prepared according to their respective tasks while preserving the connection between source recordings, annotations and extracted features.

Preparation included label checks, annotation review and manual clipping of selected recordings. Gloss annotations identified sign content, while temporal annotations identified the portions of recordings associated with that content. Automatic activity trimming removed surrounding inactivity from isolated-sign inputs. Where a recording provided a known gloss sequence without manually timed boundaries, alignment assigned that sequence to candidate intervals. These assigned intervals served as training supervision and were distinguished from manually annotated timing.

Signer-disjoint separation kept a person's isolated-sign recordings within the assigned split, preventing those recordings from appearing in both training and evaluation. This supports evaluation across signers rather than repeated exposure to the same person (Desai et al., 2023). Sequence studies retained their documented recording groups. Validation and designated tuning results guided model selection, while the English-generation study used its documented held-out sessions. Technical system evaluation and participant evaluation remained distinct activities.

### Input Preparation and Training Controls

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Move current 4.3.2 and Table 10 here. Preserve every existing preparation/training row; the augmentation details below supplement the existing augmentation entry.

Landmark preparation expressed movement relative to the body and used a common scale, normally shoulder width, with a palm-based fallback where appropriate. Image proportions were preserved, and missing landmarks retained presence indicators. Candidate sign intervals were sampled into 32 frames containing 61 landmark points with five values per point: body-relative X and Y, a relative scale-based depth proxy, presence and confidence. The resulting input had dimensions of 32 × 61 × 5; intervals of different durations could therefore be represented in a common input format.

MobileCLIP2 supplied complementary features from cropped hand images. These features preserved appearance information that might not be fully represented by landmark coordinates, while the landmark sequence represented motion and relative configuration (Jiang et al., 2021; Faghri et al., 2025).

Training incorporated controlled variations in landmark orientation, position, scale, temporal sampling and visibility. Mirroring exchanged the corresponding left and right anatomical indices. Dropout and weight decay provided additional regularization, while isolated-sign replay and reference predictions helped preserve vocabulary recognition during interval adaptation (Srivastava et al., 2014; Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019).

*Table. Landmark Augmentations Used During Adaptation*

| Augmentation | Applied setting | Intended variation |
|---|---|---|
| Camera roll | Rotation within −12° to +12° | Mild in-plane camera-orientation changes |
| Horizontal mirroring | 50% probability, with left/right anatomical indices exchanged | Mirrored signing inputs |
| Uniform scaling | 0.88–1.12 times the landmark XY coordinates | Differences in spatial scale |
| Translation | Up to ±0.04 in normalized X and Y coordinates | Differences in position |
| Coordinate noise | Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.008; 60% trigger probability | Small coordinate perturbations |
| Temporal warping | Resampling rate of 0.82–1.18; 50% trigger probability | Differences in temporal sampling |
| Landmark dropout | Removal of 2–6 landmark nodes; 25% trigger probability | Missing landmark observations |

These transformations operated on landmark inputs while preserving the associated vocabulary labels. Missing-value indicators were maintained. Candidate start and end positions also varied during interval training to prepare recognition for imprecise segment boundaries.

### Recognition and Boundary-Model Development

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Move current 4.3.3 and Figure 9 here, including the boundary-distillation and CTC discussions and citations.

The landmark recognizer used part-wise processing to represent anatomical regions before global temporal processing combined their movement. Squeezeformer integrates attention and convolution, allowing recognition to consider relationships across frames together with nearby motion patterns (Kim et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023). Hand-image representations formed a complementary temporal branch, and a learned fusion classifier combined the recognition evidence.

The combined recognizer was first fine-tuned on individual sign segments from phrase recordings. Interval fine-tuning then prepared it for candidate segments encountered during connected signing. A further interval-training variant incorporated partial-sign examples and very short fragments to discourage confident guesses on incomplete gestures. Both interval variants started from the phrase-adapted checkpoint. Isolated-sign examples and reference predictions were retained during adaptation to support vocabulary recognition.

Boundary detection was developed separately from vocabulary classification. A four-layer Transformer learned timing predictions through knowledge distillation from the frozen Sign Language Segmentation teacher. The teacher's CNN–Transformer implementation used MediaPipe pose inputs and was pretrained on German Sign Language; the ATLAS student learned to produce temporal evidence from its landmark inputs (Moryossef et al., 2023; Sign Language Processing, 2026). The teacher supplied training guidance and was not an additional runtime component in the mobile application.

The researchers also examined Connectionist Temporal Classification, which learns output sequences without requiring a boundary annotation for every sign (Graves et al., 2006). In the tested window-based implementation, the first full-window prediction required approximately 1.07 seconds of input before processing. Predictions could change as additional input arrived, and live observations included repeated output for a held sign. These implementation findings supported the use of explicit boundary estimation and stable gloss acceptance. They do not establish that CTC is inherently slow across implementations.

### English-Generation Development

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Move only the training-method part of current 4.3.4 here. Move its export/deployment content and Figure 10 to 4.4.2.

T5-efficient-tiny was fine-tuned to map gloss sequences to English through text-to-text learning (Raffel et al., 2020). The application supported incremental generation so that English could be prepared as glosses accumulated. Completed sentences were retained after consistency checks, and message completion triggered finalization of the remaining text. The English-generation component was evaluated separately from recognition so that errors in sign classification were not confused with errors in sentence generation.

## 4.3 Description of the Prototype

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Introduce the required prototype heading. Existing Chapter 3 system descriptions and Figures 1–2 stay in Chapter 3.

ATLAS is a mobile application that recognizes a selected 100-sign ASL vocabulary and produces English text and local speech from accepted gloss sequences. The prototype connects camera input, visual feature extraction, sign recognition, temporal boundary detection, sequence decoding and English generation within one application. The following subsections describe the equipment, selected processing components and user-facing operation of the prototype.

### 4.3.2 Final Models and Processing Components

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Move the already adjusted current 4.3.5 Final System Configuration here; retain its component roles. Use the component table below as an aid rather than repeating the full deployment-export table.

Visual extraction obtains structured hand, face and upper-body landmarks from device-camera images. MobileCLIP2-S0 encodes cropped hand images to provide complementary appearance information. The landmark extractor and image encoder therefore describe different aspects of the same signing input.

The final sign recognizer combines an adapted part-wise and global Squeezeformer landmark branch with a temporal Squeezeformer hand-image branch through a learned fusion classifier. Its selected configuration incorporates interval fine-tuning with partial-sign and rejection examples. Transition-refined evidence is used selectively for the evaluated sign transitions while the primary recognition output is retained.

A separate four-layer Transformer boundary detector predicts whether a frame is outside a sign, at its beginning or within it. Its knowledge-distilled timing estimates guide candidate sign intervals. The boundary detector supplies temporal evidence; the recognizer supplies vocabulary predictions for those intervals.

The segmental decoder combines boundary estimates, recognition scores and movement evidence to select and accept signs. Ordering and acceptance rules support the resulting gloss sequence, which is maintained in the visible gloss buffer. The decoder and gloss manager are processing components rather than separately trained neural models.

A fine-tuned T5-efficient-tiny model converts accepted glosses into English. Its encoder and decoder are computational parts of the same language model. Incremental generation prepares output as glosses accumulate, while the message-completion control finalizes the remaining text for display and local text-to-speech output (Raffel et al., 2020).

*Table. Final Prototype Components and Responsibilities*

| Component | Selected model or mechanism | Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Landmark extraction | Landmark extractor | Obtain structured motion inputs from camera frames |
| Hand-image encoding | MobileCLIP2-S0 | Represent cropped-hand appearance |
| Sign recognition | Part-wise/global landmark Squeezeformer, temporal hand-image branch and learned fusion classifier | Score candidate vocabulary signs |
| Boundary detection | Knowledge-distilled four-layer Transformer | Estimate temporal sign states and support candidate intervals |
| Sequence decoding | Segmental decoder with ordering and acceptance rules | Select and accept a sequence of glosses |
| Gloss management | Application processing and visible buffer | Preserve the accepted sequence for display and English generation |
| English generation | Fine-tuned T5-efficient-tiny | Produce English from the accepted gloss sequence |
| Speech output | Local text-to-speech service | Read the finalized English output aloud |

### 4.3.3 Application Features and Operation

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Reuse the existing application descriptions and verified screenshots. Do not remove the system diagrams from 4.2 or duplicate the Chapter 3 recognition timeline.

The application provides Live, Practice, Glosses and History functions. Live captures signing and presents recognition feedback, accepted glosses and generated English. Practice supports interaction with the selected vocabulary, while Glosses provides access to the supported signs. History allows saved sessions to be reviewed.

During Live operation, camera frames supply landmark and hand-image inputs. The boundary detector and recognizer provide evidence for candidate intervals, and the segmental decoder accepts a gloss sequence. English generation operates on the accumulated glosses. The Finish control signals message completion, after which the application finalizes the remaining text and provides local speech output.

The application presents predictions within the selected vocabulary. Recognition is limited to the selected vocabulary and remains sensitive to the quality and completeness of the captured input. User-facing feedback allows the recognized sequence to be inspected during the interaction.

### 4.5.1 Recognition Model Selection and Development

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Move current 4.4.1–4.4.2, Tables 11–12 and Figure 11 here. Move the base-training Figure 14 from current 4.4.7 into the base-selection discussion and retain the short eight-epoch fine-tuning explanation.

The base study compared landmark-recognition configurations before adaptation for connected signing. The flat Transformer achieved 95.50% validation accuracy, the flat Squeezeformer achieved 95.77%, and the part-wise and global Squeezeformer achieved 96.83%. These results supported selecting the part-wise and global checkpoint for subsequent development. The configurations differ in their historical training recipes; the comparison supports the selected checkpoint rather than general architectural superiority.

*Table. Base Landmark Classifier Comparison*

| Classifier | Parameters | Validation accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|

| Flat Squeezeformer | 6.47 M | 95.77% |

M denotes millions of parameters. These results concern the original base-study validation inputs before subsequent adaptation.

![Base landmark classifier training](review_assets/20261013_current_lineage/base_training.png)

*Figure. Base Landmark Classifier Training*

The base landmark classifier was trained for 130 recorded epochs. The selected checkpoint at epoch 100 achieved 96.83% validation accuracy and supplied the starting model for subsequent adaptation. Selection followed validation performance rather than assuming that the final epoch would provide the strongest recognition.

During use of the model, the researchers observed that signing with the camera slightly tilted could lead to incorrect classifications. Subsequent fine-tuning therefore included mild camera-roll augmentation within ±12°, together with the other landmark augmentations. These transformations exposed the recognizer to small orientation changes while preserving the intended sign labels. The adapted landmark model achieved 95.27% validation accuracy and maintained 94.87–95.32% across the sampled landmark rotations within ±12°.

The adapted recognition components were evaluated using the same isolated-sign validation inputs. The base-classifier comparison retained its original study membership. Landmark-only recognition achieved 95.27%, compared with 67.20% for the separate hand-image branch. Combining the two inputs reached 95.40%, a gain of 0.13 percentage points over landmarks alone. This indicates a modest aggregate benefit from adding appearance information to landmark motion.

![Recognition component accuracy](review_assets/20261013_current_lineage/component_accuracy.png)

*Figure. Isolated-Sign Validation Accuracy*

The combined recognizer was fine-tuned using sign segments from phrase recordings, producing 95.22% isolated-sign validation accuracy. Further fine-tuning on candidate intervals produced 95.20%. The phrase-to-interval difference corresponded to one additional net classification error. These adaptations prepared recognition for connected-sign inputs while largely maintaining isolated-sign performance.

A further interval-training variant started from the same phrase-adapted checkpoint and incorporated partial-sign examples and training to discourage confident recognition of very short fragments. This variant became the current primary recognizer and achieved 95.50% validation accuracy. The initial interval configuration and current primary recognizer are therefore separate fine-tuning variants from the phrase-adapted model, rather than consecutive checkpoints in one uninterrupted run.

The current primary recognizer completed eight planned fine-tuning epochs. ### 4.5.2 Connected-Sign Recognition Performance

**Placement note — not manuscript text:** Move current 4.4.3, Table 13 and Figure 12 here. Keep the five-stage WER text together; move its separate participant/technical-evaluation provision intact to 4.5.5.

Connected-sign recognition requires both vocabulary classification and identification of the intervals to be classified. The distilled boundary detector supplies temporal evidence, the recognizer scores candidate signs, and the segmental decoder selects and accepts the gloss sequence. Their combined behavior determines sequence errors that isolated-sign accuracy alone does not capture.

Evaluation during system development compared each recording's predicted gloss sequence with its reference sequence. WER counts substitutions, deletions and insertions, with lower values indicating fewer errors. The following table summarizes five development stages on the same phrase recordings.

*Table. Recognition Performance Across Development Stages*

| Development stage | WER (%) |
|---|---:|
| 1. Before interval adaptation | 35.84 |
| 2. After interval adaptation | 29.65 |
| 3. Runtime baseline | 17.70 |
| 4. Partial-sign training | 15.49 |
| 5. Transition and runtime refinement | **12.83 (retained)** |

Before interval adaptation, the phrase-adapted recognizer recorded a development WER of 35.84%. Fine-tuning on candidate sign intervals reduced WER to 29.65% under the same training-experiment evaluation procedure. The current primary fine-tuning variant also reached 29.65% under that procedure while providing the isolated-sign result reported in Section 4.5.1.

The runtime baseline integrated the trained recognizer with the distilled boundary detector and segmental decoder, recording 17.70% WER following review of training inputs and model refinements. Runtime processing used its own boundary estimation, candidate selection and acceptance rules. The change from 29.65% to 17.70% therefore crosses evaluation procedures and is not attributed to recognizer fine-tuning alone.

Partial-sign training addressed incomplete gestures encountered during connected signing. With the accompanying runtime settings, this configuration recorded 15.49% WER. Under those same settings, the configuration without partial-sign training recorded 18.58%.

Further development addressed sign ordering and acceptance, reaching 12.83% WER. Transition-example fine-tuning subsequently retained this result while addressing the evaluated transition cases.
